CHAPTER 20

The digital-twin compartment schema

How the visualiser knows what it is allowed to draw — and why that is a scientific question, not a design one.

The desktop workbench renders a live compartment view. It does not hard-code the anatomy: it reads a schema from the model, because the three backends have different state vectors and a viewer that assumed one would silently mislabel the others. Three distinctions in that schema are load-bearing.

kind
pool (mass) and concentration are physical contents and may be drawn as a filled volume. effect states are dimensionless or rate-valued — drawing one as a tank would imply a substance the model never tracks. legacy exists only so old snapshots restore.
provenance
canonical = from the published equations; extension = added by this project. A view that renders an extension alongside a canonical state without saying so is misrepresenting the model.
rate_expression
The flux term exactly as it appears in the ODE. Deliberately not reduced to a constant, because most fluxes are state-dependent — \(x_1 Q_1\) is not a rate constant.

Instantaneous flux snapshot

For live numbers the model re-evaluates its own ODE right-hand side at the state the integrator finished on, recording each transfer term into a dictionary. This is a deliberate design choice with a real property: the displayed numbers come from the same expressions that produced the trajectory, not from a second implementation that could drift out of step. Recording only copies subexpressions that are computed anyway, so an integration with recording enabled is numerically identical to one without it.

i

A snapshot is an instantaneous rate at one instant, never a mass transferred over the preceding step. The integrator evaluates the RHS several times per step, so the two are different quantities. A test pins the recorded flux set against what the ODE actually writes, so the schema cannot drift away from the equations unnoticed.

Likely jury questions

Your visualiser shows a liver. Where is it in the equations?

It is not a compartment — there is no hepatic state. It is drawn as a flux proxy fed by endogenous_production, and the inspection card says explicitly that EGP is a boundary source term with no reservoir behind it. The MPC cost function

MPCAsymmetric horizon cost
$$J(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big[ \underbrace{w_{\pm}(G_k)\,e_k^2}_{\text{tracking}} +\underbrace{5000\cdot\mathbb{1}[G_k<70]\,(70-G_k)^2}_{\text{hypo barrier}} +\underbrace{250\cdot\mathbb{1}[G_k<100]\,u_k^2}_{\text{dose-when-low penalty}} +\underbrace{0.5\,u_k^2}_{\text{regularisation}}\Big]$$ $$e_k = G_k - G_{\mathrm{target}},\qquad w_{\pm}(G_k)=\begin{cases}50 & e_k<0 \ \ (\text{below target})\\ 1 & e_k\ge 0\end{cases}$$
Horizon 60 min at 5-min steps (N = 12). Predictions come from a cloned Bergman model seeded with the current state.

The weights encode a clinical value judgement, and it is worth being able to defend it explicitly: being 50 times more averse to undershooting than overshooting, plus a barrier term that is a further 100× steeper below 70 mg/dL. This is not symmetric tracking, and it should not be — hypoglycaemia is acutely dangerous while equivalent hyperglycaemia is not. The dose-when-low penalty is separate and does something subtly different: it discourages giving insulin at all when glucose is already under 100, even if the horizon prediction looks acceptable.

△

Model mismatch is deliberate. The MPC predicts with a Bergman model while the patient may be running Hovorka. That is realistic — a real controller never has the true plant — and it means MPC results here include structural mismatch, not just parameter error. Do not present MPC performance as an upper bound on what optimal control could achieve.

A worked negative result

Textbook PID gains are a good illustration of why a bench like this exists. With \(k_p = 0.1\) U per mg/dL of error, a patient sitting at 130 mg/dL against a 120 target receives 1 U every five minutes — 12 U/h — and the outcome is not subtle.

Likely jury questions

Your PID controller fails badly. Isn't that a flaw in your platform?

It is the platform working. Those are the untuned textbook gains that ship as a teaching example, and the correct outcome for them is catastrophic hypoglycaemia — a proportional gain of 0.1 U per mg/dL is enormous for this plant. What the bench adds is that the failure is reported as a termination with a reason, not averaged into a summary statistic. I would be far more worried about a simulator in which that configuration scored acceptably.

Why is the hypoglycaemia penalty 5000 and not some other number?

It is a chosen weight, not a derived one, and I would not defend the specific value — only the ordering of magnitudes. What matters is that it is large enough that no realistic hyperglycaemia term can outweigh a predicted excursion below 70. If a jury wanted a principled version, the honest route is a risk-space cost built on LBGI (Chapter 25), which has a published basis, rather than hand-picked quadratic weights.

CHAPTER 22

AI and decision support

Where machine learning is allowed to act, and the four gates it passes through first.

Predictors estimate glucose 30 minutes ahead and can trigger a preventive response. They cannot deliver insulin, and every prediction passes a gate chain that fails closed to a deterministic heuristic. The results frame records which path was taken on every single step — predictor_used, predictor_gate_reason — so a run can be audited for how often the AI was actually trusted.

MC-dropout uses an isolated seeded generator. Its mean or standard deviation can trigger a fallback, but a stochastic sample is never delivered as an insulin result. Randomness informs a gate; it never becomes a dose.

Language models

A local LLM can explain results and formulas. Its boundary is enforced by construction rather than by prompt discipline alone: it receives the formula registry as immutable context with the instruction "Use these pre-registered formulas as immutable context. Do not derive or solve formulas," and the exported context carries the flag ai_formula_authority: false. Each formula's own policy field reads: "LLM may explain only; never derive, solve, or alter this formula."

!

The clean answer. No number that reaches an actuator in this SDK was produced by a language model. Physiology is deterministic Python; controllers are deterministic; the supervisor is deterministic; ML may propose but never dispose.

Likely jury questions

How do you know your predictor isn't confidently wrong on out-of-distribution data?

I don't know it, so the design does not depend on knowing it. Two independent gates run: an uncertainty gate on MC-dropout spread, and an OOD gate on per-feature z-scores with a breadth criterion so a single odd feature does not trip it but a broadly unfamiliar input does. Either one failing reverts to the deterministic heuristic, and the reason is written to the results frame so the fallback rate is measurable rather than assumed.

Is this "AI for diabetes"?

No, and I'd resist that framing. It is a mechanistic simulator with strictly bounded machine-learning components. The interesting research question here is not whether a network can predict glucose — it can — but what governance structure lets you use one without giving it authority it should not have. That is what the gate chain and the authority order are.

CHAPTER 23

Loss functions and optimisation

Training objectives that weight errors by danger rather than by frequency.

Plain MSE optimises for the common case. In glucose data the common case is euglycaemia, so a plain-MSE forecaster becomes most accurate exactly where accuracy matters least. Every loss here corrects that.

L1Band-weighted MSE
$$\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i w_i\,(\hat y_i-y_i)^2,\qquad w_i=\min\!\Big(w_{\max},\;1+w_{lo}\,\mathbb{1}[\ell_i<70]+w_{hi}\,\mathbb{1}[\ell_i>180]\Big)$$
Defaults \(w_{lo}\)=2.0, \(w_{hi}\)=1.5, \(w_{\max}\)=5.0. \(\ell_i\) is the glucose level, not the target.
L2Physiology-informed penalty (PINN-style)
$$\mathcal{P}=10\left[\overline{\mathrm{relu}(20-\hat y)^2}+\overline{\mathrm{relu}(\hat y-600)^2}\right] +5\left[\overline{\mathrm{relu}(|\dot{\hat y}|-\rho_{\max})^2}\right]$$ $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{band}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{P}, \qquad \lambda=0.5,\ \ \rho_{\max}=3\ \mathrm{mg/dL/min}$$
Bounds 20–600 mg/dL are the declared simulator support envelope, not a diagnostic boundary.
L3Quantile (pinball) loss
$$\mathcal{L}_q=\overline{\max\big(q\,(y-\hat y),\;(q-1)(y-\hat y)\big)}$$
For probabilistic forecasting; \(q\)=0.9 gives a conservative upper prediction band.
△

A subtle failure the code guards against explicitly. When a model predicts a delta rather than a level, applying a level threshold to the raw delta is meaningless — a 5 mg/dL drop is not hypoglycaemia, and almost no change is above 180 or below 70. Every sample would land in the "low" band, producing a constant weight, which is plain MSE with a rescaled learning rate: nothing raises, the run simply stops doing what its config says. The losses take an anchor_index and reconstruct the level from the last observed glucose before applying any threshold, and raise if the anchor is configured but the window is missing.

Likely jury questions

Is this really a physics-informed neural network?

Not in the strict sense, and the class name is historical — it is retained for checkpoint compatibility, which the docstring says. A true PINN penalises the residual of a governing differential equation. This penalises violations of declared bounds and rate limits: glucose outside 20–600 mg/dL and rates beyond 3 mg/dL/min. It is a constraint regulariser informed by physiology, and calling it anything grander would overstate it.

Why not penalise the IOB and COB relationships too?

That was considered and deliberately excluded — the comment in the source explains why. Rules like "glucose must fall when IOB is high" are not universal: stress, exercise, illness and unannounced meals all break them. Penalising a non-universal rule would teach the model to be wrong precisely in the situations that matter most.

55 mg/dL per 5 min
How large a CGM step may be before it is rejected as an artefact. It must be permissive, or it discards real physiology.
Fail-soft follow
20 mg/dL per 5 min
How fast the reported value may follow a reading that was already rejected. It must be tight, or injected sensor corruption reaches the algorithm in one step.

Likely jury questions

What makes the supervisor "independent"?

It is a separate object with its own copy of the safety configuration, it runs after the algorithm has produced its output, and it has no access to the algorithm's internal state or reasoning. An algorithm cannot disable it, weaken it, or pass it a justification. The simulator records every intervention with a reason and a latency, and there is an explicit supervisor-off ablation arm in the benchmark so its contribution can be measured rather than assumed.

Are these thresholds clinically validated?

The clinical thresholds — 70 and 54 mg/dL, and the 70–180 target range — are the international consensus values. The engineering envelopes — 15 U per bolus, 20 U per hour, the 90 mg/dL guard — are configurable defaults chosen to permit ordinary meal coverage with the bundled carb ratios while still bounding an unbounded actuator command. They are not patient-specific prescriptions, and the source says so.

CHAPTER 25

Metrics and the glucose risk space

Why time-in-range alone is an inadequate summary, and what to use instead.

RISKKovatchev symmetrisationcanonical
$$f(\mathrm{BG})=1.509\left[\left(\ln \mathrm{BG}\right)^{1.084}-5.381\right],\qquad r(\mathrm{BG})=10\,f(\mathrm{BG})^2$$ $$\mathrm{LBGI}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{f_i<0} r_i,\qquad \mathrm{HBGI}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{f_i>0} r_i,\qquad \mathrm{BGRI}=\mathrm{LBGI}+\mathrm{HBGI}$$
Both indices divide by the total count of valid readings, not the count on that branch. Averaging over one branch only would inflate both.

The blood-glucose scale is highly skewed: the distance from 120 down to 40 mg/dL is clinically far larger than the distance from 120 up to 200, but numerically it is smaller. The Kovatchev transform fixes this. Two details in the implementation are worth knowing because they are easy to get wrong:

  • The factor 10 is not cosmetic. It is what makes \(r(\mathrm{BG})\) span [0, 100] over 20–600 mg/dL, which every published LBGI/HBGI reference range assumes. Dropping it shifts every value by an order of magnitude — and therefore across the published risk categories. A test pins \(r(20) = r(600) = 100\) for exactly this reason.
  • The euglycaemic centre is derived, not assumed: \(f = 0\) at \(\exp(5.381^{1/1.084}) = 112.5\) mg/dL. It is computed from the constants rather than hard-coded, so the two can never disagree.
  • Non-positive and non-finite readings are dropped, not clipped — the logarithm is undefined there, and substituting a value would invent data.

Likely jury questions

Your algorithm reaches 80% time in range. Is that good?

It clears the consensus target, but on its own it is not enough to say. The figure I would give alongside it is time below 70, which for that run is 0.0%, and LBGI, which is 0.11 — minimal risk. The reason to insist on both is visible in the benchmark: the clean arm reports 90.3% TIR and 6.45% below 70, and the second number makes the first one not a success. A high TIR does not compensate for excessive time below range.

Can you report an HbA1c?

Only GMI, which is a linear estimate from mean glucose and is explicitly not a laboratory HbA1c. It ignores red-cell lifespan and glycation-rate variation between individuals, which is why the same mean glucose can correspond to meaningfully different measured HbA1c. In a simulation it is doubly indirect, and I would report mean glucose and TIR in preference.
