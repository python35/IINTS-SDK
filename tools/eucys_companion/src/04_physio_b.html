Insulin action

Three channels, three time constants, three sensitivities. This is where every "sensitivity" knob in the SDK ultimately lands.

What it represents

Insulin in plasma does not act instantly on tissue. It must cross the capillary endothelium, bind its receptor and trigger a signalling cascade. Each channel is a first-order low-pass filter on plasma insulin, and the three have genuinely different biology behind them — hepatic suppression is fast and highly sensitive, peripheral transport is slow, peripheral disposal is intermediate. Splitting them is what allows the model to be right about when insulin acts, not just how much.

Why three channels

Because insulin's two jobs have different dose–response curves. Note the sensitivities: \(S_{IE}\) is roughly 10× larger than \(S_{IT}\) and 63× larger than \(S_{ID}\). Physiologically this says the liver responds to far lower insulin concentrations than muscle does — half-maximal EGP suppression arrives at about 9.6 mU/L, near basal levels, while meaningful peripheral disposal needs a post-prandial peak. A single-channel model has to pick one of those behaviours and be wrong about the other.

Behaviour

SIT ↑ (transport)
Glucose moves faster into \(Q_2\). Lowers the peak of an excursion but stores glucose rather than removing it — some returns via \(k_{12}Q_2\).
SID ↑ (disposal)
Genuine removal from the body accelerates. This is the channel that determines total glucose lowering.
SIE ↑ (EGP)
Liver shuts off at lower insulin. Dominates fasting and overnight glucose; barely visible post-prandially because EGP is already floored at zero.
ka,i ↑
Faster on and faster off. Note \(k_{b,i} \propto k_{a,i}\), so the steady-state action \(k_{b,i}I/k_{a,i} = S_i \cdot M_{\mathrm{sens}} \cdot I\) is unchanged — \(k_a\) sets timing only, never magnitude. A frequent misreading.
Strongly coupled
All three share the single multiplier \(M_{\mathrm{sens}}\), so stress, exercise, dawn resistance and the clinical ISF move all three together. Only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{muscle}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{liver}}\) act on one channel each.
Edge case
\(A_{\mathrm{affinity}}\) is hard-capped at 2.0 and rejected if negative — an unbounded affinity scalar would let a configuration produce arbitrarily strong insulin.
△

Where the honesty line sits. The channel topology and the \(k_a\)/\(S_I\) values are literature-based. The three scalars — overall, tissue-specific and molecular affinity — are explicit IINTS scenario assumptions. The registry states plainly that they "are not inferred from AlphaFold confidence or ClinVar labels and require independent calibration before quantitative interpretation." If asked whether a structure-prediction score sets insulin sensitivity here: it does not, and the source says so.

\(p_2 = 0.025\) min⁻¹, \(p_3 = 5\times10^{-6}\) (mU/L)⁻¹min⁻². Insulin sensitivity in the minimal model is \(S_I = p_3/p_2\).

Because \(S_I = p_3/p_2\), the SDK applies dawn resistance and exercise to \(p_3\) rather than to \(X\) directly — scaling \(p_3\) scales sensitivity itself, while scaling \(X\) would only scale the momentary effect and would be undone at the next step.

Likely jury questions

What happens if insulin sensitivity halves?

Concretely, from the runs in Chapter 27: taking ISF from 50 to 30 mg/dL/U on an identical scenario moves mean glucose from 145 to 206 mg/dL and time-in-range from 80% to 33%, with time above 180 rising from 20% to 68%. Structurally, halving sensitivity halves every \(k_{b,i}\), so all three steady-state actions halve — but the effect is not symmetric. Hepatic suppression is on the flat part of its curve, so it loses least; peripheral disposal loses most. That asymmetry is why the excursions widen more than the fasting level rises.

Why is \(S_{IE}\) 63 times larger than \(S_{ID}\)? That looks like a typo.

They have different units, so they are not directly comparable as numbers — \(S_{IT}\) and \(S_{ID}\) are L/mU/min and \(S_{IE}\) is L/mU, because \(x_3\) is a dimensionless fraction while \(x_1, x_2\) are rate constants. The physiological content is nonetheless real: the liver is far more insulin-sensitive than skeletal muscle, and half-maximal EGP suppression sits at roughly basal insulin concentration.

Is \(\rho_{\mathrm{basal}}\) a fudge factor?

It is a declared reconciliation, and the source says why. The published Hovorka parameters describe a population; the SDK's public API accepts a patient-specific basal rate. \(\rho_{\mathrm{basal}}\) is found by bisection so that the configured basal is a genuine fasting steady state at the reference ISF. The alternative would be to hide a large startup transient in the initial conditions, which is worse: it would look like physiology.

The liver is a boundary flux, not a compartment. There is no hepatic glycogen state in this model. EGP appears as a source term on \(dQ_1/dt\) with no reservoir behind it, so it never depletes. In the desktop app's digital-twin view the liver is drawn as a flux proxy and labelled as such, precisely so nobody reads it as a stored quantity.

Original unit conversion

The published value is in molar units; the SDK works in mass. The conversion is applied once, in the parameter definition, and is worth being able to reproduce on a whiteboard:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{EGP}_0 &= 16.1\ \frac{\mu\mathrm{mol}}{\mathrm{kg\cdot min}} \times 0.18\ \frac{\mathrm{mg}}{\mu\mathrm{mol}} = 2.898\ \frac{\mathrm{mg}}{\mathrm{kg\cdot min}}\\ F_{01} &= 9.7\ \frac{\mu\mathrm{mol}}{\mathrm{kg\cdot min}}\times 0.18 = 1.746\ \frac{\mathrm{mg}}{\mathrm{kg\cdot min}} \end{aligned}$$
Glucose molar mass is 180.16 g/mol, so 1 µmol ≈ 0.18 mg. At 70 kg: EGP₀ = 202.9 and F₀₁ = 122.2 mg/min.

A useful sanity figure: 202.9 mg/min ÷ 112.0 dL is about 1.8 mg/dL per minute, or roughly 109 mg/dL per hour, if nothing opposed it. Basal insulin exists to cancel almost exactly this.

Glucagon interaction
\(x_{\mathrm{gluc}}\) is added inside the max, so glucagon can lift the liver back off the floor even under full insulin suppression. That is the mechanism a bi-hormonal controller exploits.
Likely jury questions

Why is there no glycogen store? Surely the liver can run out.

It can, and that is a stated limitation. Over the 24-hour horizons this bench runs, hepatic glycogen depletion is not the binding constraint — but for a multi-day fast or prolonged endurance exercise it would be, and the model would over-produce glucose. Adding a glycogen compartment would need a depletion and repletion rate that this SDK has no data to calibrate, so the honest choice was to leave it out and say so rather than invent one.

How can you have a hepatic term when you have no portal-vein insulin?

We do not model the portal circulation, so hepatic and systemic insulin are the same variable \(I\); the hepatic/systemic distinction is carried entirely by the separate sensitivity \(S_{IE}\) and the \(\sigma_{\mathrm{liver}}\) scalar. That is a first-order approximation of hepatic selectivity, not a two-compartment portal model, and a ratio like \(S_H = I_{sys}/I_{pv}\) is not something this model can compute.

What it represents

The brain and red blood cells consume glucose continuously and do not need insulin to do it. That draw is roughly constant at normal glucose, but below about 81 mg/dL (4.5 mmol/L) transport into tissue becomes concentration-limited and the uptake falls proportionally. This branch is canonical Hovorka, and it is one of the most important safety features of the model.

Why it matters more than it looks

At 81 mg/dL, NIMGU is the single largest glucose sink in the fasting state — 122 mg/min against an EGP of 203 mg/min. If it stayed constant as glucose fell, a hypoglycaemic excursion would accelerate: the lower you go, the faster you fall. The proportional cut-off makes it decelerate instead. Together with F11's counter-regulatory rise in EGP, this is why the model has a natural floor rather than diverging.

Behaviour

Exercise starts
\(H_{\mathrm{ex}}\) rises with a 10-minute time constant; GLUT4 then follows with its own ~20-minute rise. Uptake therefore lags the start of exercise by roughly half an hour.
Exercise stops
Asymmetric by design: \(k_{\mathrm{deact}} = 0.01\) is five times slower than \(k_{\mathrm{act}}\), so elevated uptake persists for hours. This reproduces post-exercise late hypoglycaemia.
G below 81
NIMGU falls linearly to zero at G = 0. The largest sink self-limits.
Bounded
GLUT4 is confined to [0,1] by construction; a value above 1 fails the step rather than being clipped.
△

GLUT4 here is a latent scenario state, not a molecular measurement. The literature supports exercise-mediated insulin-independent uptake; the activation constants and the 1.5 multiplier are IINTS heuristics. The registry says so explicitly: it is "not measured receptor abundance or a cell-level translocation assay". Do not present it as proteomics.

Likely jury questions

Why call it GLUT4 if it is not a GLUT4 measurement?

Fair criticism, and the naming is a compromise between readability and precision. The variable is named after the mechanism it abstracts, but the source docstring, the compartment schema and the formula registry all mark it as an extension and a heuristic. If a jury pushes on it, the correct answer is that it is a bounded exercise-uptake state whose name is suggestive and whose parameters are assumptions.

Why is the knee at 81 mg/dL specifically?

It is 4.5 mmol/L, the canonical Hovorka low-glucose branch point, converted to mg/dL. It is not a tuned value — it comes straight from the published model, and the comment in the source records both the mmol/L original and the conversion.
