CHAPTER 10

Meal and carbohydrate absorption

The other side of the timing problem: carbohydrate is also delayed, and by a different amount.

F05adapted
F05Two-compartment meal absorption
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dD_1}{dt}&=-\frac{D_1}{t_{\max,G}}\\ \frac{dD_2}{dt}&=\frac{D_1}{t_{\max,G}}-\frac{D_2}{t_{\max,G}}\\ U_G&=A_G\,\frac{D_2}{t_{\max,G}} \end{aligned}$$
Structurally identical to F04 — the same serial-depot idea, applied to the gut. A meal of \(m\) grams enters as \(D_1 \mathrel{+}= 1000\,m\) mg.

What it represents

Carbohydrate must be emptied from the stomach and absorbed across the intestinal wall before it appears as plasma glucose. As with insulin, two serial compartments give the right qualitative shape: no appearance at the instant of eating, a peak at \(t = t_{\max,G}\), and a tail. \(A_G\) is the fraction that ever arrives — the rest is not absorbed.

The mismatch factor

\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{meal}}\) multiplies the true carbohydrate the patient absorbs while the algorithm is told the announced amount. Combined with the simulator's reported_value on a meal event and the missed_meal event type, this is how the bench tests carb-counting error and unannounced meals — the dominant real-world failure mode for bolus calculators.

Behaviour

tmax,G ↑ (fat, fibre)
Flatter, later, longer appearance. Lower peak glucose but a much longer tail — the classic "pizza effect". Total absorbed is unchanged.
AG ↑
Scales the whole curve, changing the asymptote but not the shape or timing.
εmeal ≠ 1
Decouples what the patient absorbs from what the algorithm was told. This is the carb-counting-error lever.
Edge case
Meals stack additively into \(D_1\). Two meals an hour apart produce one merged appearance curve, not two resolvable peaks — correct behaviour, but it means the model cannot attribute a rise to a specific meal.
△

Two different meal chains exist. The Hovorka backend uses the published two-compartment \(D_1 \to D_2\) chain above. The Bergman and advanced backends retain a three-stage stomach → gut adaptation (\(Q_{sto1} \to Q_{sto2} \to Q_{gut}\), with \(\tau_{meal}\), \(k_{abs}\), \(f_{bio}\)) which is an IINTS adaptation and, per the registry, "requires dataset calibration". Do not present numbers from the two branches as equivalent.

Likely jury questions

Does your model distinguish glycaemic index?

Only through \(t_{\max,G}\), which is a single lumped speed. It captures fast versus slow carbohydrate but not the biphasic appearance of a mixed meal with substantial fat and protein, and there is no protein-to-glucose conversion at all. For a mixed-meal study this is a real limitation and would need the Dalla Man-style variable gastric emptying rate.

Why is bioavailability 0.8 rather than 1.0?

It is the published Hovorka value and reflects that not all ingested carbohydrate reaches the systemic circulation as glucose — first-pass hepatic extraction and incomplete absorption. It is configurable, and the figure verifies the accounting: 60 g in, 48 g absorbed.

CHAPTER 11

Renal glucose clearance

A small term with an outsized numerical importance: it is where a naive model gets a discontinuity.

F14heuristic
F14Differentiable renal clearance
$$\operatorname{softplus}_s(z)=s\,\ln\!\left(1+e^{z/s}\right),\qquad F_R = c\cdot V_{G,\mathrm{dL}}\cdot\operatorname{softplus}_s\!\left(G-162\right)$$
\(c = 0.003\), \(s = 10\) mg/dL, threshold 162 mg/dL (9 mmol/L). Bergman omits the \(V_{G,\mathrm{dL}}\) factor because it works in the concentration domain.
SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
cClearance coefficient1/min0.003
sSplay (transition width)mg/dL10.0
θRenal thresholdmg/dL162.0

What it represents

Above the renal threshold the kidneys cannot reabsorb all filtered glucose and the excess is lost in urine. Physiologically the transition is not sharp — nephrons have a distribution of reabsorptive capacities, which clinicians call splay — so a smooth function is the more faithful choice as well as the more convenient one.

Why softplus specifically

The obvious implementation is \(F_R = c\max(0, G-\theta)\). It is wrong for two reasons. Physiologically it has no splay. Numerically, its derivative jumps from 0 to \(c\) at the threshold, and an adaptive solver crossing that point repeatedly must shrink its step dramatically — expensive, and a source of step-size chatter. Softplus is \(C^\infty\), so \(dF_R/dG\) is continuous everywhere and the solver crosses the threshold without noticing.

$$\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\operatorname{softplus}_s(z)=\max(0,z),\qquad \frac{d}{dz}\operatorname{softplus}_s(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z/s}}=\sigma\!\left(z/s\right)$$
The derivative is exactly the logistic sigmoid — so the smoothed threshold has a logistic dose–response, which is what a nephron-capacity distribution would predict.
i

The implementation is a numerically stable softplus: it returns \(s\cdot z/s\) for \(z/s > 50\) and \(s\,e^{z/s}\) for \(z/s < -50\), avoiding both overflow in \(e^{z/s}\) and catastrophic cancellation in \(\ln(1+\varepsilon)\). Between those it uses log1p(exp(z)).

△

The threshold is a configurable approximation. A real renal threshold varies between individuals (roughly 160–200 mg/dL) and is altered by SGLT2 inhibitors and by renal impairment. 162 mg/dL is one value, chosen as 9 mmol/L; there is no per-patient identification behind it. A second helper, renal_glucose_clearance_concentration, defaults to 180 mg/dL for concentration-domain use — do not quote the two thresholds as one number.

Likely jury questions

Is renal clearance even significant at these glucose levels?

Modestly. At 250 mg/dL it removes about 30 mg/min against an EGP of roughly 200, so it is a real brake on severe hyperglycaemia — it is part of why glucose plateaus rather than rising without limit when insulin is withheld. Below 162 mg/dL it is essentially zero, so it plays no part in normal control.

Why not simply use max(0, G−θ)?

Two reasons, and I would give the numerical one first because it is checkable: the hard version is not differentiable at the threshold, so an adaptive RK45 integrator repeatedly rejects and shrinks steps when the trajectory hovers near 162 mg/dL. The physiological reason is that renal splay is a documented phenomenon — the sharp version is the less realistic of the two, not merely the less convenient.

CHAPTER 12

Exogenous glucagon

The second hormone. Structurally a mirror of insulin absorption, with a saturating effect compartment.

F13adapted
F13Two-depot glucagon PK and bounded PD
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dY_1}{dt}&=u_G-k_1Y_1, & \frac{dY_2}{dt}&=k_1Y_1-k_2Y_2 &&\text{(PK)}\\ \Gamma&=\frac{k_2Y_2}{w\cdot Cl_{F,C}}, & a_G&=\frac{\Gamma}{CE_{50}+\Gamma} &&\text{(saturating)}\\ \frac{dx_{\mathrm{gluc}}}{dt}&=k_{aG}\left(S_G\,a_G-x_{\mathrm{gluc}}\right) &&&&\text{(effect compartment)} \end{aligned}$$

Why a saturating effect and a separate effect compartment

Two distinct non-linearities are needed, and conflating them is a common error. The Hill/Michaelis–Menten term \(a_G = \Gamma/(CE_{50}+\Gamma)\) says receptor occupancy saturates: doubling the dose past \(CE_{50}\) does not double the effect. The effect compartment \(x_{\mathrm{gluc}}\) says the effect also lags the concentration, because the hepatic response takes time. One is about magnitude, the other about timing.

△

Split evidence. The two-state PK and the clearance output follow published T1D glucagon parameter ranges. The bounded effect compartment coupling into EGP is an unvalidated IINTS adaptation. Say which half is which if asked — the registry does.

Likely jury questions

Your simulated glucagon peaks near 3000 pg/mL — is that physiological?

It is far above endogenous levels, which sit in the tens of pg/mL, but this is a 1 mg pharmacological rescue dose, not endogenous secretion, and supraphysiological peaks are expected. What the figure really shows is that at that dose the effect term is saturated, so the model is insensitive to the exact peak — which is reassuring, because the peak is the part of the PK we are least confident about.

Does the model include endogenous glucagon secretion?

Not as a hormone with its own kinetics. Endogenous counter-regulation is represented separately and more crudely by the rescue multiplier F11, which lifts EGP directly as glucose falls. The Γ state here is exogenous glucagon only. Merging the two would be the natural next extension.

CHAPTER 13

Counter-regulatory hypoglycaemia response

The body's own defence — and the state that can switch it off.

F11heuristic
F11Endogenous rescue multiplier
$$\Delta_{\mathrm{hypo}}=\max(0,\,70-G),\qquad a=\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{hypo}}}{16+\Delta_{\mathrm{hypo}}},\qquad R_{\mathrm{rescue}}=1+a\,(1-\mathrm{HAAF})$$
Multiplies EGP₀ inside F06. Bounded above by 2 when HAAF = 0.
SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
θhypoActivation thresholdmg/dL70.0
khalfHalf-activation deficitmg/dL16.0
SmaxMax fractional EGP increase—1.0
HAAFExposure memory, blunts the response—state

What it represents

As glucose falls below 70 mg/dL a healthy autonomic response releases adrenaline and glucagon, and the liver increases output. The functional form is again a saturating Michaelis–Menten term: the response ramps up over the first tens of mg/dL of deficit and then plateaus. Half-maximal activation is at a 16 mg/dL deficit — that is, at 54 mg/dL, which is exactly the clinical Level 2 hypoglycaemia threshold. That alignment is deliberate.

Behaviour

G above 70
\(\Delta = 0\), \(a = 0\), multiplier exactly 1. The term is completely inert in normal range — no hidden contribution.
G = 54
\(a = 0.5\); EGP is raised by 50% if HAAF = 0.
G → 0
\(a \to 1\); EGP at most doubles. The response is bounded, so it cannot rescue an arbitrarily large insulin overdose.
HAAF → 1
\((1-\mathrm{HAAF}) \to 0\) and the multiplier collapses to 1 regardless of how low glucose goes. The defence is gone.
Likely jury questions

Is this a validated model of counter-regulation?

No, and the registry says so: it "captures the concept of blunted counterregulation; not a diagnostic HAAF model". It has the right direction, the right saturation and a clinically meaningful half-activation point, but the coefficients are not identified from clamp data. It should be read as a mechanism that prevents the model from diverging in hypoglycaemia, not as a prediction of any individual's response.

Why cap the response at a doubling of EGP?

Because an uncapped counter-regulation would make the simulator unfalsifiable — any insulin overdose would eventually be rescued, and no controller could ever be shown to be dangerous. Bounding it at \(S_{max} = 1.0\) means a sufficiently large overdose still produces severe hypoglycaemia, which is exactly the outcome the bench needs to be able to report.

CHAPTER 14

Hypoglycaemia-associated autonomic failure

A memory state with two very different time constants — the only place the model remembers days rather than hours.

F12heuristic
F12Antecedent-hypoglycaemia exposure memory
$$s=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{70-G}{70-54},\,0,\,1.5\right),\qquad \frac{d\,\mathrm{HAAF}}{dt}=\underbrace{\frac{s\,(1-\mathrm{HAAF})}{360}}_{\text{build}}-\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{HAAF}}{4320}}_{\text{recovery}}$$
Severity width is the gap between the awareness (70) and severe (54) thresholds, so \(s = 1\) at exactly 54 mg/dL.
SymbolMeaningUnitDefaultInterpretation

Why the asymmetry is the whole point

\(\tau_{\mathrm{recover}} / \tau_{\mathrm{build}} = 12\). Impairment accumulates in hours and resolves over days. That single ratio is what makes repeated mild hypoglycaemia progressively dangerous rather than merely repetitive, and it is the clinically observed pattern the state is abstracting. A symmetric state would be pointless — it would simply track current glucose.

Bounded by construction
The \((1-\mathrm{HAAF})\) factor makes build vanish as HAAF → 1, and the linear decay makes it fall whenever severity is zero. HAAF cannot leave [0,1] without a numerical fault.
Equilibrium
Steady state under constant severity: \(\mathrm{HAAF}^{*} = s/(s + 360/4320) = s/(s+0.0833)\). Even \(s = 0.1\) — glucose at 68.4 mg/dL — equilibrates at HAAF ≈ 0.55.
Enforcement
Material excursions outside [0,1] fail the step. Only solver-scale tolerance is projected back — the model does not quietly clip a physiological error.
!

Never report HAAF as a clinical finding. The registry's validation note is explicit: "Research memory state only; never report as clinical hypo-awareness diagnosis." Impaired hypoglycaemia awareness is diagnosed with validated instruments such as Gold or Clarke scores, not by a simulator state.

Likely jury questions

Where do 6 hours and 3 days come from?

They are order-of-magnitude choices consistent with the clamp literature — antecedent hypoglycaemia blunts counter-regulation within the same day and recovery takes days to weeks — but they are not fitted values. The source docstring says the constants are "deliberately explicit so they can be calibrated against clamp-study data instead of being hidden in model code". That is the strongest honest claim: the structure is right, the numbers are a starting point.

Why does severity cap at 1.5 rather than 1.0?

So that glucose below the severe threshold still builds memory faster than glucose at it — the cap is reached at 46 mg/dL. Without a cap, an extremely low excursion would dominate the entire history; without any headroom above 1.0, severity would be flat across the whole Level-2 range, which would lose real information.
