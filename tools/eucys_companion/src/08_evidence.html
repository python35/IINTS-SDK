What this supports

  • The SDK can execute a large locked matrix over profiles, scenarios, algorithms, arms and seeds.
  • Clean and deliberately corrupted data conditions produce measurably different outcomes.
  • Algorithm and safety-event trade-offs are exported in reviewable tables.
  • A result can be traced back to protocol and run artefacts.
  • The platform exposes undesirable outcomes instead of hiding them.

What it does not support

  • That any candidate algorithm is clinically safe.
  • That a high TIR compensates for excessive time below range.
  • That every supervisor intervention is appropriate.
  • That the virtual population represents a clinical population.
  • That the same performance would occur on an external dataset or a certified simulator.
  • That the simulator can be used to determine a real dose.
!

Raise this before the jury does. The clean arm reports 6.45% mean time below 70 mg/dL. The consensus target is under 4%. That is not a result to present as satisfactory control, and it is the single most important open finding in the benchmark. It requires investigation by profile, scenario, algorithm and seed.

Note also the counter-intuitive pattern: the corrupted arm has less time below range (3.69%) than the clean arm. That is not the corruption helping — it is that corruption pushes runs hyperglycaemic (23.12% above 180), which mechanically reduces time below. Reading the two numbers together is the point.

Likely jury questions

Your best arm has 6.45% time below range. Isn't that dangerous?

Yes, it is above the consensus limit of 4%, and I flag it as the benchmark's principal open finding rather than defending it. What I would not do is average it away — the arm's 90.3% TIR looks excellent and is not, and that is precisely the failure mode this platform is built to expose. The next step is disaggregation by profile and algorithm to establish whether it is concentrated in specific configurations or systemic.

Why does turning the supervisor off improve TIR?

Because the supervisor's job is not to maximise TIR — it is to prevent unsafe delivery, and every intervention it makes costs some in-range time. The ablation arm reaching 80.78% against the corrupted arm's 73.19% is expected. The comparison to read is against the clean arm, and the honest reading is that this single table cannot establish that every one of the 177 mean interventions was appropriate. That is listed under what the benchmark does not support.

Is 3600 runs enough?

It is enough to characterise the matrix that was specified, with 10 seeds per cell. It is not enough to make a population claim, because the six profiles are not a sample of any real population — they are configurations. Increasing seeds narrows the confidence interval around a number whose external validity is the actual limitation.

* The basal setting is not a pure dose — and this is worth raising yourself. basal_insulin_rate does two things at once. It sets the delivered basal, and it is also the calibration input to _derive_basal_parameter_scale, which back-solves \(\rho_{\mathrm{basal}}\) by bisection so that the configured basal is a genuine fasting steady state.

Measured directly from the model: \(\rho_{\mathrm{basal}}\) = 1.231 at 0.3 U/h, 0.462 at 0.8 U/h and 0.264 at 1.4 U/h. A higher configured basal therefore describes a more insulin-resistant patient, and the resistance outweighs the extra insulin — which is why raising it raises mean glucose. That is intended behaviour, documented in the source, and it is a genuine trap for anyone reading the parameter as a dose knob.

Method and its limits

Design
One-at-a-time (OAT) around a single nominal point, ±25%, identical seed and scenario so differences are attributable to the parameter alone.
What OAT cannot find
Interactions. Reduced hepatic and reduced peripheral sensitivity together is not the sum of the two rows above, and OAT will never show that.
What OAT cannot find
Non-local behaviour. The ICR row is already non-monotone within ±25%; a larger perturbation could reverse the sign of a gradient measured here.
Honest next step
A variance-based global method — Sobol indices over a joint parameter distribution — would give first-order and total-effect indices including interactions. That is not implemented here, and I would not claim OAT as a substitute.
Likely jury questions

Have you done a proper global sensitivity analysis?

No. What is shown is a one-at-a-time screen around a single nominal point, which identifies the dominant parameter and — usefully — flags one non-monotone response. A proper global analysis would be Sobol or Morris over a joint distribution, giving total-effect indices that capture interactions. OAT cannot see interactions at all, so I would present these as a screening result, not a variance decomposition.

Which parameter would you most want measured in a real patient?

Insulin sensitivity, unambiguously — it has the largest span in the screen and it is the one whose error is asymmetric in risk. The second would be the carb ratio, not because its span is large but because its response is non-monotone, so an adaptive controller tuning it on a mean-glucose objective could move in the wrong direction and still see the metric improve.

Mathematical and methodological limitations

  • Parameter identifiability. Nineteen states and dozens of parameters cannot be identified from glucose and insulin inputs alone. Several — the tissue scalars, the affinity scalar, the HAAF time constants — are set, not fitted.
  • Population parameters, individual claims. The Hovorka values describe a population. \(\rho_{\mathrm{basal}}\) reconciles a patient-specific basal against them, which is a declared approximation.
  • Sensitivity screening only. No variance-based global analysis; interactions are unmeasured.
  • Model mismatch in MPC. The controller predicts with Bergman against a possibly-Hovorka plant. Realistic, but it means MPC results are not an optimal-control upper bound.
  • The 6.45% low-glucose burden in the clean benchmark arm is unexplained and above target.
  • Nine of sixteen formulas are heuristic. Their coefficients require calibration before any quantitative interpretation.

Likely jury questions

What is the single biggest weakness of your project?

Parameter identifiability. The model's structure is defensible and mostly published, but a nineteen-state system has far more parameters than the available inputs can identify, so several are asserted rather than fitted. That is why the SDK marks nine of sixteen formulas heuristic in the source itself, defaults every extension to inert, and separates mathematical correctness from experimental validation everywhere it reports. The honest position is that this is a well-instrumented bench with a partly-calibrated plant.

What would you need to make this clinically credible?

Three things, in order. First, parameter identification against clamp or well-characterised CGM data, at minimum for insulin sensitivity and the meal chain. Second, external validation — replay real patient days and compare predicted against observed trajectories, reporting the error rather than tuning it away. Third, cross-validation against a certified simulator on a shared protocol. None of that is done, and no amount of additional simulation substitutes for it.

Why should anyone trust results from an uncalibrated model?

They should not trust the absolute numbers, and I don't ask them to. What survives an uncalibrated plant is comparative and structural findings: that corrupted input costs 17 percentage points of TIR, that an inflow-only dawn model is dose-cancellable and therefore flatters a controller, that a sensitive patient is at higher risk than a resistant one at equal parameter error. Those depend on the model's structure and sign conventions, which are defensible, not on its exact coefficients.
